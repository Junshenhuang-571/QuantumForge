# Quantum routes to PDEs: Schrödinger-equation simulation, nonlinear lifting, and hardware feasibility

Research notes, primary sources only. Scope: (1) the Schrödinger equation as the original PDE-to-circuit connection, including Hamiltonian-simulation algorithms and stated resource estimates; (2) nonlinear PDE/ODE routes — Lloyd-style multi-copy nonlinear evolution, Carleman linearization/lifting, reaction–diffusion extensions, and quantum-walk / Fourier routes for wave-type equations — together with published criticisms and conditioning/error caveats; (3) hardware feasibility: what resource estimates and actual NISQ experiments report, with circuit depths, qubit counts, and the cost of full-state readout. This document is research only: it makes no recommendations and no build plan.

All claims below were read from arXiv preprints or peer-reviewed articles directly (search/extraction date: November 2026). Numbers are reported as stated by the source; "demonstrated" and "proposed/estimated" are kept separate.

## 1. Schrödinger equation as the original PDE-to-circuit connection

Hamiltonian simulation is the task of compiling evolution under a Schrödinger equation, \(e^{-iHt}\), into gates. Qubitization implements this for a block-encoded \(H\) with query complexity \(\mathcal{O}(t+\log(1/\epsilon))\) to both oracles — stated as optimal in all parameters, asymptotically and non-asymptotically — using at most two additional ancilla qubits [9]. A 2018 PNAS resource study of explicit circuits found that, for a 50-qubit system, the Taylor-series algorithm used 171 qubits, the quantum-signal-processing (QSP) algorithm 67, and the product-formula (PF) algorithm 50; segmented QSP improved on the rigorously bounded PF algorithm by about an order of magnitude in CNOT count and almost two orders of magnitude in T count, while empirical error bounds improved PF by two to three orders of magnitude [10]. The same study notes that empirical PF beat full QSP by about an order of magnitude in CNOT count, so the ranking depends on whether rigorous error bounds are required [10].

The Schrödinger-equation structure is also used deliberately to *convert* other PDEs into simulatable form. Schrödingerization applies a warped phase transformation that recasts any linear — including non-autonomous — ODE or PDE as Schrödinger-type equations in one higher dimension, in real time [19][8]. For ill-posed problems the extra qubits for the extended dimension become almost \(\log\log(1/\epsilon)\) for target precision \(\epsilon\), and the paper states \(\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(dT/\epsilon)\) queries and \(\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(d^2T/\epsilon)\) additional two-qubit gates for its stated setting [8]. A circuit-level implementation gives explicit QFT and Trotter constructions on \((dn_x+n_p)\) qubits and states an overall heat-equation cost of \(\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(d^2T^2\|u(0)\|^3\|u(T)\|^3h^4/\epsilon^3)\) single-qubit and CNOT gates; its numerical tests are simulator-only and real-hardware implementation is listed as future work [16].

Costa, Jordan and Ostrander (arXiv:1711.05394) use the most direct version of this connection for the wave equation: discretizing gives \(\mathrm{d}^2\phi/\mathrm{d}t^2 = -(1/a^2)L\phi\), and choosing \(H=(1/a)\begin{bmatrix}0&B\\B^\dagger&0\end{bmatrix}\) with \(BB^\dagger=L\) makes a subspace of a Schrödinger evolution obey the discrete wave equation [21][18]. They state \(\log_2[(1+D)(\ell/a)^D]\) qubits, state-preparation time \(\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(D^{5/2}\ell/a)\), Hamiltonian-simulation time \(\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(tD^2/a)\), and gate count \(g=\mathcal{O}[\tau(n+\log^{5/2}(\tau/\epsilon))\log(\tau/\epsilon)/\log\log(\tau/\epsilon)]\) with \(\tau=s\|H\|_{\max}t\) [21]. Their classical comparison baseline is that any algorithm outputting a full field description must scale at least as \(\Omega((\ell/a)^D)\) [21]. The output is a quantum state proportional to the solution; measuring it samples intensity (squared amplitude), not the field vector itself [21].

## 2. Nonlinear PDE and ODE routes

### 2.1 Lloyd-style nonlinear evolution with multiple copies

Lloyd, De Palma, Gokler, Kiani, Liu, Marvian, Tennie and Palmer (arXiv:2011.06571, v2 2020) propose encoding the state as a quantum state and implementing polynomial nonlinearities with multiple copies \(x(0)^{\otimes n}\), combining nonlinear-Schrödinger dynamics with quantum linear differential-equation solvers [2]. They state that resources grow quadratically in integration time and logarithmically in state-space dimension, with the copy number required to exceed the number of time steps and to be "significantly greater than \(T\)" in regimes where Lyapunov exponents are not too large [2]. This is a proposal with stated algorithmic scaling, not a hardware demonstration [2]. Motivation and caveat both appear in the literature: generic nonlinear modifications of quantum mechanics would solve unstructured search in time polynomial in \(\log n\), which is why efficient simulation of general nonlinear dynamics is expected to be difficult [1].

### 2.2 Carleman linearization / lifting to a linear system

The Liu–Kolden–Krovi–Loureiro–Trivisa–Childs algorithm (PNAS 118, e2026805118, 2021; arXiv v4 2026) maps dissipative quadratic \(n\)-dimensional ODEs to an infinite-dimensional linear system via Carleman linearization, truncates it, and solves with forward Euler plus a quantum linear system algorithm [1]. Under \(R<1\), where \(R\) is the ratio of nonlinearity plus forcing to linear dissipation, its stated complexity is \(T^{2}q\,\mathrm{poly}(\log T,\log n,\log(1/\epsilon))/\epsilon\), with \(q\) measuring solution decay; this was an exponential improvement over previous algorithms exponential in \(T\) [1]. The same paper proves a worst-case lower bound making the problem intractable for \(R\geq\sqrt{2}\), notes that exponential decay in \(T\) precludes efficiency because of the \(q\) factor, and says the Navier–Stokes case is reachable only provided the Reynolds number is sufficiently small [1].

For nonlinear PDEs, An, Fang, Jordan, Liu, Low and Wang (arXiv:2205.01141, v2 2023; Communications in Mathematical Physics) adapt the approach to reaction–diffusion equations using an \(\ell_\infty\)-based condition \(R_D<1\) [11]. They state that \(R_D\) is independent of the number of spatial grid points \(n\) while \(R\) grows with \(n\), so the \(R_D<1\) criterion can remain convergent under grid refinement; applications are Fisher–KPP and Allen–Cahn, with mean-square kinetic energy estimated by post-processing the output state [11].

Subsequent work targets the cost drivers rather than the embedding alone. An et al. (arXiv:2312.09518, 2023) report logarithmic dependence of complexity on error and near-linear dependence on time via a high-precision linear solver, a rescaling technique that removes a cost exponential in Carleman order which would otherwise prevent quantum speedup for PDEs, and tighter Carleman error bounds [15]. The same paper states that the expected exponential speedup in discretization points is generally *not* realized for PDEs because discretization norms or condition numbers reduce this to a polynomial speedup, and that a proposed preconditioner was later found not to significantly reduce the condition number [15]. Wu and Wang (arXiv:2405.12714, v2 2025) derive convergence beyond strong dissipation under a no-resonance condition, with convergence rate \(\mathsf{R}_r=\mathcal{O}(\mu\kappa_1(W)\|F_2\|_1/\Delta)\), while noting that the earlier lower bound suggests that outside the dissipative regime Carleman linearization may not converge and quantum algorithms generally will not offer an advantage [14].

### 2.3 Published criticisms and quantitative caveats for nonlinear lifting

Lewis, Eidenbenz, Nadiga and Subaşı (arXiv:2307.09593, v2 2024) close the \(1\leq R<\sqrt{2}\) gap in the worst-case bounds: they construct coupled differential equations allowing state discrimination in integration time \(\mathcal{O}(\log(1/\epsilon))\) for states with overlap \(1-\epsilon\); since state discrimination requires \(\Omega(1/\epsilon)\), any algorithm outputting a state approximating the normalized solution vector must have complexity exponential in integration time for systems with one or more positive Lyapunov exponents and sub-exponentially growing solutions [3]. They state this makes an efficient general algorithm for chaotic regimes unlikely, while explicitly not excluding polynomial speedups for some tasks [3].

Lin, Lowrie, Aslangil, Subaşı and Sornborger (arXiv:2202.02188, v3 2024) analyze the necessary finite-dimensional projection and report that it induces errors that can be hard to control: for their scalar example the Carleman solution is bounded only for \(|R|<1\), blows up for \(R\geq1\) after \(t>-\frac{1}{r}\log(1-|R|^{-1})\), and for \(\varepsilon x_0>0\) blows up after \(t>1/|x_0\varepsilon|\), while the exact solution may remain bounded [4]. They conclude that a practical, reliable and accurate general method remains open and that the useful time horizon is often short [4]. A multi-chart "global" Carleman study (arXiv:2510.15715, 2025) reports that chart-to-chart remapping requires nonlinear operations, that exponential speedup in compression and work is lost at remapping for generic chaotic systems, and that the CFL-type constraint \(\Delta t<\tau/P\) can reduce the quantum speedup to polynomial at best when \(P\gg n\) [13].

### 2.4 Quantum-walk / Fourier routes for wave-type equations

The Costa–Jordan–Ostrander construction is described in the comparative literature as a direct quantum-walk approach with \(O(d\log N_h)\) qubits and \(\mathrm{polylog}(N_h)\) depth [24]. A resource study of that solver (Suau et al., arXiv:2003.12458, v2 2021) implements the algorithm "from scratch," checks gate counts, qubit counts and precision against the predicted asymptotics, and reports a 1-D wave simulation with \(N_d=32\) discretization points \(n=6\) qubits, target precision \(\epsilon=10^{-5}\) and an observed absolute error of order \(10^{-7}\) against a classical finite-difference solver [5]. Crucially, the circuits were *not* run on hardware: the authors note that IBM Q Melbourne had 14 usable qubits but their circuits required more, and they estimate that the number of physical qubits may be three to four orders of magnitude above the logical count [5]. Other comparative entries in the same 2026 trapped-ion paper list a QFT+QSVT route for smooth fields with depth \(\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(d^3K^2n_h t/\epsilon)\), and explicit finite-difference Hamiltonian circuits with depth \(\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(dn_h^3T^2/\epsilon)\) [24].

## 4. Full-state readout is the end-to-end bottleneck

Every amplitude-encoded route above produces a quantum state, not a classical vector. One 2024 paper states plainly that reading out the full classical solution by tomography generally scales exponentially with system size and is prohibitive, and notes that even measuring a \(k\)-local observable can remove the exponential advantage of the linear solve [7]. A QSVT/linear-systems pathway paper lists full tomography as an option that "sacrifices exponential speedup," with sampling or observable estimation as the alternatives [22]. A cross-paradigm heat-equation benchmark gives the concrete accounting: reconstructing \(N\) amplitudes to per-component precision \(\epsilon\) is partial state tomography with a \(1/\epsilon^2\) Monte Carlo factor, and computational-basis sampling of the full field costs \(\mathcal{O}(N/\epsilon^2)\) shots [12]. In its example at \(n=7\) (\(N=128\), \(\epsilon=10^{-2}\)), total thermal energy needed \(\mathcal{O}(10^4)\) shots per time step versus \(\mathcal{O}(10^6)\) for the full field; with \(10^5\) shots per step HHL degraded to about 0.79 relative \(\ell_2\) error, and the authors explicitly decline to claim full-field advantage [12]. In \(d\) dimensions with \(N=2^{nd}\) grid points, an \(\Omega(N)\) readout overhead removes the exponential-in-\(d\) advantage suggested by evolution complexity alone [7][12]. The finite-projection critique makes the same point from the dynamical-systems side: the state must be sampled many times to extract information [4].

## 5. Primitives required by this literature

Factual inventory only, drawn from the papers above; no recommendation is implied.

- **Encoding / state preparation:** amplitude encoding of field or solution vectors; variational brickwall / hardware-efficient ansätze for initial conditions; classical-to-quantum loading of Gaussian, Ricker and polynomial profiles [6][20][21].
- **Fourier and sparse structure:** QFT and inverse QFT for Fourier-space PDE solvers and hypergraph incidence factorizations of discretized Laplacians [21][24].
- **Hamiltonian simulation:** product formulas/Trotter–Suzuki; Taylor series; qubitization; QSP/QSVT over block-encoded Hamiltonians; sparse-Hamiltonian simulation oracles [9][10][21].
- **Linear algebra primitives:** block encoding of sparse/tridiagonal/Laplacian matrices; quantum linear-system algorithms; QSVT matrix inversion; multi-grid or preconditioner constructions; post-selection flags and success-probability amplification [1][15][22].
- **ODE/PDE time stepping:** forward Euler and higher-order/time-marching schemes; implicit discretizations; linear-combination-of-Hamiltonian-simulation-style evolution; Richardson extrapolation; warped phase transformation with an auxiliary momentum register and recovery from a chosen slice [1][8][15].
- **Nonlinearity handling:** multi-copy tensor-product registers for polynomial terms; Carleman embedding, truncation order \(N\), and dissipativity or \(\ell_\infty\)-based conditions [1][2][11].
- **Advanced embedding variants:** rescaling to avoid cost exponential in Carleman order, no-resonance conditions, and multi-chart remapping [13][14].
- **Variational/hybrid components:** parameterized circuits, loss functions from solution values and derivatives, parameter-shift gradients, classical optimizers (e.g. SLSQP), parallel shot stacking, and local stencil micro-kernels inside a classical time loop [17][23].
- **Output and measurement:** projective measurement of position/bitstring probabilities, observable expectation values, amplitude estimation, full or shadow tomography, and ML/QCNN-based readout [7][12][20].
- **Fault-tolerance accounting:** T/Toffoli counts, magic-state assumptions, logical-to-physical qubit multipliers, two-qubit gate depth under device connectivity, and coherence-time comparisons [5][10][22].

## 6. Limitations and criticism (consolidated)

- **Regime restrictions:** the flagship Carleman result needs \(R<1\) and non-exponential solution decay; its worst-case lower bound starts at \(R\geq\sqrt{2}\), subsequently tightened to \(R\geq1\) for algorithms that output the normalized solution state [1][3].
- **Finite-time blow-up and uncontrolled projections:** truncation to a finite lifted space can diverge at analytically characterized times even when the exact solution stays bounded; reliable long-horizon prediction is not established [4].
- **Speedup erosion:** PDE discretization condition numbers generally reduce dimension speedup to polynomial; full-field readout costs \(\Omega(N)\) samples [7][12][15].
- **Hardware gap:** resource studies for block-encoding/QSVT routes give two-qubit depths from \(10^5\) to \(10^6\) for small (7–11 qubit) instances and state explicitly that these exceed current coherence limits; successful device demonstrations use shallow or variational circuits and limited observables/grid sizes rather than full amplitude-encoded PDE solutions [17][22][24].
- **Multi-chart cost:** where Carleman embedding is globalized across charts, nonlinear remapping and CFL-type step restrictions can erase the exponential advantage [13].
- **Scope of negative results:** the lower bounds apply to algorithms of the stated form (outputting a state approximating the normalized solution vector), and the authors of those bounds explicitly leave open polynomial advantage for restricted tasks such as mean-field properties [3].
